# Ultrathink Editorial Analysis

## Mission and Editorial Standard

Ultrathink is a weekly AI newsletter for modern executives. Its ambition is to become the #1 applied-AI newsletter for business decision-makers by combining timely analysis with practical ways to lead an AI-native enterprise.

A successful issue should leave the reader with:

- A sharper view of an important AI development.
- One practical idea worth testing, discussing, or assigning.
- A small set of additional signals worth following.

The publication should feel informed and opinionated without assuming the reader follows AI discourse all day.

## The Established Three-Act Format

The current structure is strong and should remain the default:

1. **`future proof`:** A provocative take on the most strategically interesting development of the week.
2. **`ai native`:** The main applied column and clearest expression of the brand promise.
3. **`worth your time`:** A concise roundup of useful developments and links.

The opening greeting and “Today, we’re talking about” preview make the issue approachable. The Tenex services CTA separates Acts 1 and 2, while the job board closes the issue.

Do not routinely reverse Acts 1 and 2. Act 1 earns attention and makes the newsletter shareable. Act 2 creates retention by delivering practical value. The goal is to adjust their weight, not replace the sequence.

## What Already Works

- The three-act rhythm is easy to understand and visually distinctive.
- The issues are well researched, timely, and the right overall length.
- Headlines are memorable and usually contain a clear point of view.
- The voice is direct, energetic, and willing to reject conventional wisdom.
- The best passages turn complicated developments into vivid conclusions, such as “Price in the parking lots. Leave the driveways for later.”
- The mix of analysis, application, and links feels both snackable and substantial.

## What the Three Examples Show

| 1 | Open source versus frontier labs | Routing work among models | 443 | 445 | Act 1: 7.5/10. Act 2: 5.5/10 |
| 2 | Renting versus owning the AI stack | Running a model bake-off | 387 | 455 | Act 1: 6.5/10. Act 2: 5.5/10 |
| 3 | Tesla’s power infrastructure as an AI moat | Building a self-checking loop | 571 | 287 | Act 1: 9/10. Act 2: 3.5/10 |

Word count alone understates the imbalance. Act 1 often contains longer paragraphs, more entities, more statistics, and more argumentative turns. Example 1 is balanced by length but not by cognitive effort. Example 3 is heavily dominated by Act 1.

Example 2 is the strongest overall model. Its first act raises a legitimate executive question about whether to rent or own the AI stack. Its second act gives readers a credible, inexpensive way to test model choice against real work. The two acts serve complementary purposes without needing to share one theme.

## Priority 1: Rebalance Attention Toward Act 2

`ai native` is the main meal. It is where Ultrathink most clearly fulfills its applied-AI promise, so readers should reach it with attention remaining.

Target approximately:

- **Act 1:** 35–45% of the combined reading effort.
- **Act 2:** 55–65% of the combined reading effort.
- **Act 1 length:** usually 350–425 words.
- **Act 2 length:** usually 400–550 words.

These are guides, not rigid quotas. A major story may justify a longer Act 1, but it should be an exception.

To make Act 1 easier to read:

- Advance one argument rather than including every available proof point.
- Reduce the number of companies, people, models, and statistics the reader must remember.
- Establish the prevailing narrative, provide the strongest counterevidence, explain the overlooked factor, and reach the executive implication.
- Prefer shorter paragraphs. Avoid blocks of 100 words or more.
- End after the conclusion instead of continuing to accumulate evidence.

Example 1 occasionally feels compressed and formulaic. Example 3 flows more naturally but is simply too dense and long for Act 1’s role.

## Priority 2: Be Less “Timeline Native”

The newsletter sometimes assumes readers recognize every company, model, controversy, and social-media debate. Executives should not need to follow AI discourse daily to understand the argument.

Add only the context required to answer:

- What happened?
- Why is this different from ordinary AI news?
- What decision, risk, or opportunity might it affect?

Avoid lengthy backstory, but define unfamiliar actors and explain the stakes on first reference. A useful item should produce a durable conclusion, not merely summarize the week’s conversation.

The research and synthesis are strong, but many insights currently reset with the news cycle. Build editorial memory gradually by referring back to earlier conclusions, updating prior calls, and repeating durable principles when new evidence supports them. Named proprietary frameworks can emerge later. They are not a prerequisite for useful work today.

## Priority 3: Clarify the Job of Act 2

The three existing Act 2s focus on model routing, model evaluation, and coding loops. They are practical, but they lean toward sophisticated AI users and technical operators. The opportunity is to translate technical ideas one organizational level upward without pretending to possess enterprise experience the writer has not earned.

Act 2 should leave the reader with at least one of the following:

- A low-risk experiment that can be completed within a week.
- A workflow worth examining.
- A decision method or evaluation criterion.
- A reported example of how another organization is using AI.
- A question an executive should take to a team meeting.

Whenever possible, identify:

- **Decision:** What choice does this affect?
- **Owner:** Who should investigate or act?
- **Workflow:** Where does the idea apply?
- **Evidence:** What result would indicate that it works?
- **Boundary:** When would the advice not apply?

The writer should write from earned authority:

- Use “we tested” only for work the team actually performed.
- Attribute practitioner experience rather than presenting it as the writer’s own.
- Distinguish an observed result from a hypothesis that may generalize.
- Offer modest experiments instead of unsupported enterprise playbooks.

If the writer lacks direct access to business leaders, Act 2 can still succeed through first-hand product tests, interviews with Tenex practitioners, public case studies, and clearly attributed operating examples.

## Act 2 Topic Territories

Broaden the section beyond coding tools while staying close to verifiable evidence:

- **Workflow selection:** Which repeated tasks are suitable for AI?
- **Evaluation and economics:** How should teams compare quality, cost, speed, and required edits?
- **Adoption:** What small experiment can create useful employee behavior?
- **Governance:** Where are review, permissions, or accountability required?
- **Organization:** Which role should own a pilot or recurring AI process?
- **Tool use:** What can the team personally test and document?

Example 2 provides the best current template: choose a real task, run a controlled comparison, judge the result by work required, record the outcome, and repeat later.

## Act-by-Act Drafting Blueprint

### Opening

- Greet the reader.
- Lead with one surprising fact or short anecdote.
- Interpret it in one or two sentences.
- Preview the three main contents.

The opening anecdote should be quick and entertaining. Do not let it compete with Act 1.

### Act 1: `future proof`

1. State the surprising development or prevailing belief.
2. Explain the strategic question underneath it.
3. Present the strongest evidence.
4. Separate what is known from company claims and Ultrathink’s inference.
5. Conclude with the implication for business leaders.

Act 1 does not need a tactical checklist. Its job is to improve judgment.

### Act 2: `ai native`

1. Start with a recognizable work problem.
2. Explain the relevant change in plain language.
3. Show what was tested, observed, or reported.
4. Give the reader a small method, experiment, or decision aid.
5. Include a limitation or condition.
6. End with a specific “Try it” action.

Act 2’s job is not to prove the writer can use advanced tools. Its job is to make an applied AI idea useful to the ICP.

### Act 3: `worth your time`

- Include roughly three to five items.
- Lead each item with the editorial conclusion, not a generic headline.
- Separate that opening hook from the summary with one em dash.
- Explain what happened and why it matters.
- End with the source.
- Remove items that are interesting but have no clear consequence for the reader.

## Voice and Style

Preserve:

- Direct, conversational language.
- Specific numbers and concrete examples.
- Strong conclusions and memorable metaphors.
- Occasional fragments for pace.
- Clear labels such as “Our take” and “Try it.”

Newsletter copy must sound written and edited by a human. Never use semicolons. Avoid em dashes in narrative copy, with one deliberate exception: use one between the hook and summary in each `worth your time` item. Use periods, commas, or colons elsewhere. Favor natural transitions over rhetorical templates.

Watch for AI-assisted writing patterns:

- Repeated “it’s not X, it’s Y” constructions.
- Strings of short dramatic fragments.
- Artificially symmetrical lists or contrasts.
- Rephrasing the same conclusion several times.
- Overconfident transitions unsupported by the evidence.
- Too many proper nouns added to demonstrate research.
- Abstract declarations such as “That distinction matters” when the next sentence can make the point directly.

AI assistance is not itself a problem. The final edit must remove formulaic rhythms and restore a natural authorial voice.

## Recommended Editorial Process

Before drafting, write one sentence for each act:

- **Act 1:** “The reader will understand that…”
- **Act 2:** “The reader will be able to…”
- **Act 3:** “These signals matter because…”

Then:

1. Research and source each consequential factual claim.
2. Draft for completeness.
3. Cut Act 1 to its essential argument.
4. Break up dense paragraphs and remove unnecessary entities.
5. Test Act 2 against the decision-owner-workflow-evidence-boundary questions.
6. Remove repeated AI-shaped rhetorical patterns.
7. Check that the roundup adds signal rather than volume.
8. Read the issue once as an executive who does not follow AI every day.

## Final Quality Checklist

- Does Act 1 make one clear argument?
- Can a non-expert follow it without knowing the week’s online discourse?
- Does Act 2 receive more editorial weight than Act 1?
- Does Act 2 offer a credible action, method, or reported example?
- Is the advice proportional to the writer’s evidence and experience?
- Are fact, company claim, and editorial inference distinguishable?
- Are paragraphs short enough to scan?
- Have repeated antithetical or formulaic constructions been removed?
- Does every “Worth Your Time” item explain why it matters?
- Does the issue remain recognizably Ultrathink: informed, direct, useful, and enjoyable?
